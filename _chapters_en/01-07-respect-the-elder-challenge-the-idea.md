## The reflex

In this example, an experienced salesperson proposes keeping an offer as it is. She knows the customers well. A new colleague has heard several requests that seem to contradict her reading.

The colleague hesitates to speak: she may be missing context, and she does not know how her disagreement will be received. The decision moves ahead without those observations being examined.

## The builder's reflex

> "These requests make me hesitate about the approach. What context am I missing, and how can we look at them before deciding?"

## Why

Respecting a person and examining their reasoning can go together. Their experience brings valuable information; a recent observation can also reveal a change. Neither the age of an idea nor its newness settles anything. In this example, the requests heard might signal a lasting need, an exception, or a misunderstanding. That still has to be checked.

Disagreement becomes useful when the people involved can see what is being contested, on what facts, and with what consequences. You can share a goal and argue about the means. You can also contest the goal itself: agreeing first is not a required step. An objection does not have to carry a solution already to deserve examination, especially when it flags a risk.

Whether you can speak at all depends on the conditions. A private conversation, something written, or the backing of someone trusted can help, without guaranteeing there will be no consequences. If you are exposed, looking for support or postponing a non-urgent discussion can be reasonable. An urgent alert needs a channel suited to it. It falls to the people who organise the decision to make objections receivable and to prevent retaliation.

The decision can stay the same after examination. What matters then is explaining what was taken into account, what is uncertain, and the circumstances that would justify reopening the question. A repeated objection can mean a point is still unanswered; how often it comes up is not on its own a measure of its worth.

## Try this

On an upcoming decision, prepare one fact, its possible effect, and a question. Pick a channel that fits the urgency and your exposure. Ask who can examine the point and when an answer is possible.

If you are running the discussion, say the objection back before you answer. With the people involved, note what was examined and the reason for the choice, without publicly attributing a sensitive alert. If no discussion is possible, name the support or the condition that is missing; carrying that blockage alone is not your job.

## From where you sit

- **Newcomer**: bring an observation and ask for the context that might change how it reads.
- **Experienced colleague**: explain your reasoning and what would make you revise it.
- **Decision owner**: plan a channel for answers and a way to handle objections still open.
- **Supporter**: offer your help with the agreement of the person concerned, without speaking for them.

## To discuss

In a recent disagreement, which fact did we examine, and how was the decision explained to the people affected?
