## The starting point

> "I'll wait until I understand it better before I ask."

The discussion moves on and an important word stays vague. You can follow the sentences without knowing whether you give them the same meaning as everyone else.

## The move

> "So we decide on the same basis, what do you mean by activation here?"

## Why it works

A shared word can cover several realities. Inside one team, "activation" can mean a sign-up to one person and a first use to another. A definition with an example makes the gap visible before you start comparing numbers.

The question serves every level of experience. Starting out, you learn the vocabulary and how it is used. With experience, you can spot that a definition has shifted, or that it hides several cases. Asking does not prove you were pretending to understand until now.

Pick your moment according to what is at stake. If the decision turns on the word, ask during the discussion. If the detail can wait, note it and come back to it. In a group where asking a question exposes you, look for someone you trust or use writing. Whoever is running the discussion can also ask for the example themselves, which makes the clarification easier for everyone.

This practice does not replace looking things up. An available definition may be enough; a term people use differently often needs a conversation.

## Try this

On the next vague notion, ask for a definition and a concrete case.

> "Can you show me one case that falls inside that definition, and one that falls outside?"

Say back what you understood. Before the decision, or at the next exchange, check that the definition handles the case being discussed. If the gap holds, put it in the decision note rather than keep an apparent agreement.

## From where you sit

- **Product**: give an example, with the name of the metric you use.
- **Management**: leave room for questions, and answer without grading the person who asks.
- **Customer relations**: compare the customer's words to the team's before you translate the request.
