A salesperson learns to ask a question that opens up the need. An accountant spots an inconsistency and knows how to check it. Someone in support explains a hard step without leaving the other person to guess what comes next. An engineer makes a change easier to understand and to maintain.

The craft grows in moves like these. It brings together tools, knowledge and a judgement that practice sharpens. You can work on a new skill or go deeper into something you already do well.

If you are starting out, choose a task within reach and an example you can examine. Redo a small piece, ask for feedback on one point, and go again. You do not need to master the whole field to learn something useful.

With experience, you hold bearings worth keeping. A new constraint, an unusual case, or a comparison from outside can help you see what still holds and what deserves to move. A stable practice can be a success too: learning does not require changing what works for no reason.

The cards offer several ways to get better: read a source, watch a line of reasoning, compare two approaches, look for a cause, or listen to another craft. Some are specifically about software. Others carry over to a procedure, a document or a service. Choose the one you can actually try.

Time, access to resources, and the chance to get feedback all count. If you organise the work, the ⇄ cards help you plan for those conditions, rather than leaving progress to depend on whoever has free evenings.

A sign of progress can be a decision you explain better, a mistake you now know how to spot, or a move you make with less help. Compare situations close enough that you can see what changed.

**What to explore next:** autonomy helps you use that skill on a problem whose remit still has to be pinned down. You can exercise it with the right support, without waiting to be an expert.
