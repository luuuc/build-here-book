A card can help you examine a situation and prepare an attempt together. Discussion does not guarantee a change; reading alone can be useful too. Choose the format according to the need, the people, and the means available.

Start with one session, with feedback at a moment that is relevant to the attempt. Thirty minutes is a suggested duration, not a constraint to hold at the expense of reading or understanding. You can carry on over several weeks if the feedback justifies the effort, then stop.

## Preparing a simple frame

Choose a fact you can share: a handover that stays ambiguous, feedback that is hard to use, or a question about use that needs checking. A practice that works and that you want to deepen will do too. A constructed case lets you start without exposing a file or a person.

Agree the time, who facilitates, and who keeps the short record of decisions. Preparation, taking part, and follow-up take real space in diaries. If that changes planned work, have whatever gets pushed back confirmed by the person who can rule on it.

Facilitating rests on an agreement and on backing, not on years in the job. Someone starting out can co-facilitate, keep time, or guide the reading of a prepared case. An experienced person can bring context without becoming the only one who interprets the card.

Announce the frame to the participants: anyone can pass, answer in writing, or prefer the constructed case. The session is not for assigning a builder level or for assessing people in public. Ask for agreement before keeping an attributed contribution; do not promise a confidentiality the group cannot guarantee.

The ⇄ cards can help you prepare a request. They do not give the group authority to change a rule. If the conditions call for a decision elsewhere, identify who to ask and prepare a bounded proposal.

## A thirty-minute session

**Five minutes: read and clarify.** Leave time to read the card, or to hear it read if that suits. Explain a word or an example if it is needed. The link can go out beforehand, without assuming everyone will have read it. If the text needs more time, shorten what follows or extend the session by agreement.

**Eight minutes: examine a case.** Ask: "What do we know about this situation? What already works? What is still an assumption?" Invite the people concerned to add to it, without imposing an account on them. The block *From where you sit* suggests points of view: do not speak for a craft that is not in the room. Note the question to put to them instead.

**Ten minutes: compare options.** Look for a useful attempt, the option of keeping the current practice, and each person's constraints. State the expected effect, the time, the access, and the protections. Ask what could make the attempt unsuitable. Fast agreement does not prove the practice has landed; disagreement does not prove a lack of builder spirit.

**Seven minutes: agree what follows.** Separate a decision inside the group's mandate, a proposal that needs approval, information to obtain, and closing with no action. No participant gets a responsibility without accepting it. Choose a moment when an effect can be observed, or a date for getting the answer you need.

## The facilitator's sheet

You can take these lines up before the session:

- **Purpose:** we want to shed light on ... from case ... and card ...
- **Participation:** people have been invited; they can pass or propose another form of feedback.
- **Time and backing:** preparation ... ; session ... ; follow-up ... ; work displaced and agreement ...
- **Mandate:** we can decide ... ; we have to ask ... for ...
- **Facilitation and record:** ... facilitates, ... notes what is agreed; access to the note ...
- **Acceptable outcome:** an authorised attempt, a precise request, a check, or an explicit choice not to continue.

During the exchange, use concrete prompts: "What fact lets us say that?", "Who would live with this change?", "Which agreement is missing?", "What help would make the attempt possible?" If you hold the decision authority, let the others bring their information first and say what is still open.

If a card is used to aim at a colleague, stop that use and come back to the fact or to the constructed case. If a subject calls for individual handling, offer the right channel outside the session. If someone no longer wants to take part, do not read their withdrawal as a diagnosis. If the discussion stays tense or lacks information, pausing is a possible outcome.

## A short record, with a clear status

An example of what a session produces:

> **Fact:** in the case examined, the file that went out had no explicit next action.
>
> **Proposal:** try a line reading "next action and handover accepted by" on ordinary files for one cycle.
>
> **Means:** Jo agrees to prepare an example in twenty minutes. The manager has to confirm that time and the remit before any shared change.
>
> **Limits:** keep the controls and the access rights; stop if the recipient cannot pick it up.
>
> **Status:** awaiting agreement. Lina agrees to ask for an answer by Friday. Silence is not authorisation.
>
> **Observation planned if the attempt is authorised:** check with the recipient whether they identify the next action, then note the questions and the time added.

The [log of decisions and results](/en/templates/) lets you keep that. Writing "attempt under way" while an agreement is missing would erase an important constraint.

## The feedback that justifies what follows

At the agreed moment, book a short check-in, fifteen minutes for instance: three to restate the question and the status, five for the observations, five for the decision, and two to confirm the responsibilities. Adapt that split to the case.

Ask what was actually tried, what effect was observed, and what is still unknown. Examine the effort, the help received, and the load displaced too. An action not taken can signal a missing access or capacity; it is not automatically a lack of commitment.

Choose to keep, change, stop, or check another way. Have what follows accepted, and close the responsibilities that are finished. The number of sessions and written decisions describes activity; it does not demonstrate that the service or the learning got better.

The case [Six weeks to learn together](/en/learning-as-a-team/) shows successive decisions and an account with its limits. Use a shorter or longer period according to the real cycle, without setting up a permanent meeting by default.

## Adapting to the people and the conditions

**Between peers.** Choose a case you can examine and a move inside your remit. Facilitating can rotate. To change other people's work, prepare the request with them; consensus among peers does not replace the agreement you need.

**Remote, or across different hours.** Use a note reachable in an authorised space. Agree a window for reading and responding that fits the hours people work, then someone to pull the agreements and the unknowns together. No answer is not agreement. If access or writing is a problem, plan a short conversation or another format.

**With a new team or with beginners.** Start with a constructed example, spell out the terms, and offer co-facilitation. A first session may only produce a better understanding of the case. Cut the scope rather than demanding a decision nobody is ready to carry.

**With no backing from above.** A voluntary discussion about an authorised case can still be useful. Do not launch a collective change without a mandate, and do not assume the follow-up will happen in the evening. If a request is refused, look for the constraint and, where possible, a smaller option. After a sustained refusal, pause that line of work and get it clarified what that changes for the existing commitments.

**With someone who wants to back you.** Propose bounded help: a review, an access to request, or an introduction both sides accept. That person does not have to run the session or take ownership of the project. The [backing and account templates](/en/templates/) help pin down the commitment and its end.

After the first round of feedback, ask whether the format helped and whether people want to do it again. Keeping to individual reading, a pair, or an occasional discussion can be the right next step.
