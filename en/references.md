These eighteen references let you find ideas the book draws on and examine their context. Their age does not prove everything has already been solved. They demonstrate neither a ranking of builders nor the validity of the questionnaire.

Choose a reading according to the question you want to go into. For learning, Norvig and Hamming offer starting points. For the conditions of collective work, look at Edmondson, Grove or Deming. To examine a need or a delivery, Levitt, Fitzpatrick, Cagan and *Accelerate* give different perspectives. You do not have to read everything, or buy a book, to start practising what is in this one.

The notes below separate the reference from the use proposed here. Connections to other crafts are lines of reading, not results established by these authors. Some links give the full text, others a summary, an extract, or a library record. The dates refer to the edition or release named; the linked pages may show a later edition.

**1911. Frederick Winslow Taylor, *The Principles of Scientific Management*.** A historical text on splitting the preparation of work from its execution. It helps you examine where certain prescriptions came from; its conception of authority is not a model to take up uncritically. [Full text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/6435/pg6435-images.html).

**1954. Peter Drucker, *The Practice of Management*.** A bearing for putting the work of management, its goals and its responsibilities up for discussion. Here, the connection to the cards is there to raise questions about what work is for, not to reduce every activity to a commercial enterprise. [Library record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb37570872q).

**1960. Theodore Levitt, *Marketing Myopia*.** An invitation to define the business by the needs it serves rather than by the product that exists. [The reissue, at the publisher](https://store.hbr.org/product/marketing-myopia-harvard-business-review-classics/2601).

**1968. Melvin Conway, *How Do Committees Invent?*.** The relation it proposes between how an organisation communicates and how its systems are designed can help you investigate a dependency. It is not enough to explain every architecture by the org chart. [Introduction and text by the author](https://www.melconway.com/Home/Conways_Law.html).

**1975. Fred Brooks, *The Mythical Man-Month*.** Essays on organising software development and on the cost of coordination. Adding people to a late project means examining learning and communication; the famous formula does not replace that examination. [Anniversary edition at the publisher](https://www.pearson.com/en-gb/subject-catalog/p/Brooks-Mythical-Man-Month-The-Essays-on-Software-Engineering-Anniversary-Edition-2nd-Edition/P200000000149/undefined).

**1977. Michel Crozier and Erhard Friedberg, *L'Acteur et le Système*.** A bearing for reading the constraints of collective action and the room people have to decide. The connection proposed here is to examine concrete dependencies without assuming anyone's intentions. [Record for the original edition](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb34708065n).

**1979. Donald T. Campbell, *Assessing the Impact of Planned Social Change*.** An article on evaluating social change. It invites you to examine how a measure used for deciding can influence what it claims to observe. A work metric therefore also calls for a discussion of how it gets used. [Article and DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0149-7189(79)90048-X).

**1982. W. Edwards Deming, *Out of the Crisis*.** MIT Press dates the origin of this book to 1982. Its management proposals give a place to working conditions and to learning. They do not establish a universal percentage of problems attributable to the system. [At the publisher](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262350037/out-of-the-crisis/).

**1983. Andrew S. Grove, *High Output Management*.** Management practices drawn in particular from experience at Intel. You can examine support, decisions and a team's means there, separating what a manager can decide from what a peer can propose. [At the publisher](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/72467/high-output-management-by-andrew-s-grove-former-chairman-and-ceo-of-intel/).

**1986. Richard Hamming, *You and Your Research*.** An account of choosing problems, of research conditions, and of working habits. It is a situated talk, not a measure of talent or a general rule about careers. [Transcript of the talk](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~robins/YouAndYourResearch.html).

**1992. Bruno Latour, *Aramis, or the Love of Technology*.** An inquiry into an abandoned transport project and the relationships that were supposed to hold it together. It lets you question a purely technical explanation of failure, without automatically naming a culprit. [The French edition at the publisher](https://www.editionsladecouverte.fr/aramis_ou_l_amour_des_techniques-9782707121202).

**1997. Eric S. Raymond, *The Cathedral and the Bazaar*.** An essay on modes of software development and on cooperation around open projects. The chances to learn from shared work do not imply that everyone can publish their professional work. [The author's text, revised since its first release](https://www.catb.org/esr/writings/cathedral-bazaar/cathedral-bazaar/).

**1999. Amy Edmondson, *Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams*.** The article studies 51 teams in a manufacturing company and reports an association between psychological safety and learning behaviours. It is not the study of hospital teams sometimes summarised under this reference. That association does not guarantee the effect of any particular workshop. [Article in the Harvard repository](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/13a7b031-0fdd-45ec-a7e0-2b80e2bc679f).

**Peter Norvig, *Teach Yourself Programming in Ten Years*.** An online text, kept updated, on learning through practice and feedback. The title does not set the same timescale for every person or every craft; Norvig himself points out the limits of a single number. [The author's text](https://www.norvig.com/21-days.html).

**2011. Rich Hickey, *Simple Made Easy*.** A talk distinguishing simple from easy in software design. It gives you a way of examining what is intertwined; extending it to other activities stays an analogy to check. [Talk and notes](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/Simple-Made-Easy/).

**2013. Rob Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test*.** Practices for preparing conversations and limiting biased feedback. Looking for facts rather than approval stays an aid to judgement, without making one interview a validation of the need. [The book's site](https://www.momtestbook.com/).

**2017. Marty Cagan, *Inspired*, second edition.** This edition develops the work of product teams and the discovery of useful solutions. The distinction between a problem handed over and features prescribed serves as a bearing; it does not remove a beginner's need for framing or for learning. [The author on this edition](https://www.svpg.com/inspired-v2/).

**2018. Nicole Forsgren, Jez Humble and Gene Kim, *Accelerate*.** The book presents research on software delivery performance. It helps you examine delivery, stability and working conditions together; it does not close the speed-versus-quality debate in every craft and every context. [Introduction and extracts at the publisher](https://itrevolution.com/product/accelerate/).

---

This selection is marked by management and software. It does not represent every tradition of learning, every community practice, or every local experience.
